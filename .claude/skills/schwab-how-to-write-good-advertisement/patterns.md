# Patterns: Copywriting Techniques from Schwab

## Headline Patterns

### Interrogative Headline (Question-Based)
**When to use:** When you want to awaken curiosity and pull reader into copy.

**How:** Ask a question that makes reader want to know the answer.

**Example:** "How much are you overpaying for this service?"

**Trade-offs:** High curiosity appeal; requires strong first paragraph to deliver promised answer; doesn't work for simple product benefits.

---

### Specific Numbers Headline
**When to use:** When you have quantifiable benefit; creates credibility and specificity.

**How:** Lead with concrete number: "Save 30 minutes daily", "Increase output by 40%", "In just 5 days..."

**Example:** "Earn $500 more per month with this simple technique"

**Trade-offs:** High impact; requires truthful claims; generic numbers lose power.

---

### Benefit Promise Headline
**When to use:** For straightforward products where reader benefit is clear and strong.

**How:** State the direct benefit using "you/your": "Eliminate back pain forever", "Get the promotion you deserve"

**Example:** "Your new car smell, guaranteed — no mechanic required"

**Trade-offs:** Clear and direct; lower curiosity; works best for high-need audiences.

---

### Negative Consequence Headline
**When to use:** When loss aversion is stronger motivator than gain (often true).

**How:** Highlight what reader loses if they don't act: "Don't make this mistake", "If you miss this deadline..."

**Example:** "Lose 5 years of earning potential by not reading this"

**Trade-offs:** Powerful; can feel alarmist if overused; works especially well for security-focused motivators.

---

## First Paragraph Patterns

### The Immediate Benefit Bridge
**Structure:** Start with headline's promise; immediately deliver first major benefit in 2-3 short sentences.

**Example:** 
Headline: "How to write better ads in 7 days"
Para 1: "Most copywriters struggle for months. But if you follow this exact system, you'll write your best ad by Friday. Here's why this works so fast..."

**Trade-offs:** Keeps interest high; can feel rushed if not carefully paced.

---

### The Curiosity Question Pattern
**Structure:** Open first paragraph with pertinent question that makes reader want answer.

**Example:** "Did you know that 70% of ads fail at the headline stage? But there's a proven pattern that changes those odds..."

**Trade-offs:** Engages reader; requires strong answer to avoid disappointing.

---

### The Vague Generality Killer Pattern
**Structure:** State what reader already knows is wrong ("We all know..."), then pivot to specific insight.

**Example:** "We all know that 'quality' doesn't mean much anymore. But there's one specific quality that actually moves customers to buy. It's not what you think..."

**Trade-offs:** Validates reader knowledge; positions you as insider; creates curiosity.

---

## Proof Techniques (Ranked by Strength)

### Testimonial Pattern
**When to use:** When you have customers similar to your reader.

**How:** Quote specific person (name, location) describing specific result.

**Example:** "As a busy mom in Portland, I thought I'd never find time for this. Then I discovered... and now I spend 2 hours less per week on this task." — Sarah M., Portland OR

**Trade-offs:** Highest perceived credibility when geographically close; weaker if generic.

---

### Expert Endorsement Pattern
**When to use:** When you need authority backing for claims.

**How:** Name recognized expert in field, their credentials, their endorsement.

**Example:** "Dr. James Peterson, leading cardiologist at Johns Hopkins, recommends this approach for..."

**Trade-offs:** Builds authority; requires actual expert permission; weaker if expert is unfamiliar to reader.

---

### Demonstration Pattern (Show in Use)
**When to use:** When product benefit is visual or experienced.

**How:** Describe or show product being used in real scenario.

**Example:** "Watch as this engineer completes a 30-minute job in 5 minutes using this tool. No special training. No setup time."

**Trade-offs:** Most persuasive visually; requires ability to demonstrate; harder in copy-only formats.

---

### Test Results Pattern
**When to use:** When you've measured specific outcomes.

**How:** Describe test methodology, results, quantify improvement.

**Example:** "In our laboratory test, samples treated with this formula lasted 40% longer than leading competitors, withstanding 500+ use cycles without degradation."

**Trade-offs:** Highly credible; requires actual testing; must specify test conditions.

---

### Guarantee/Risk Removal Pattern
**When to use:** When reader's hesitation is uncertainty or risk.

**How:** Offer specific guarantee (timeframe, terms, what if fails).

**Example:** "Try it risk-free for 30 days. If you don't see improvement, we refund every penny. No questions. No hassle."

**Trade-offs:** Removes buying friction; signals confidence; requires honoring guarantee.

---

### Reputation/Prestige Pattern
**When to use:** When company longevity or status is advantage.

**How:** State years in business, institutions served, scale of operation.

**Example:** "Trusted by Fortune 500 companies for over 40 years. Serving 10 million customers worldwide."

**Trade-offs:** Builds authority; only works if reputation is genuine and known.

---

## Copy Structure Patterns

### The Two-Force Pattern (Emotion + Proof)
**Structure:** Open with emotional appeal; support with factual proof throughout.

**Example:**
- Emotional: "Imagine never missing a deadline again..."
- Proof: "Our system integrates with 50+ tools, reminders arrive 24 hours before, completion tracking shows 95% on-time delivery..."

**Trade-offs:** Most effective pattern; requires both elements; can feel long if facts aren't concise.

---

### The Reader-Portrait Pattern
**Structure:** Build specific mental model of reader; write directly to their circumstances.

**Example:**
"You're a busy executive. You're smart. You've tried 5 other solutions. They all promised but underdelivered. Here's why this is different..."

**Trade-offs:** Highly personalizing; requires accurate reader understanding; fails if portrait is off.

---

### The Specific Purchase Point Pattern
**Structure:** Focus exclusively on reader benefits (purchase points), not product features.

**Not:** "This product has 50 features and weighs 2 pounds."
**But:** "You'll save 3 hours weekly and carry it easily in your bag."

**Trade-offs:** More persuasive; requires translating all features to benefits; easier for some products than others.

---

### The Subheading Momentum Pattern
**Structure:** Use frequent, bold subheadings (every 2-3 paragraphs) with curiosity or novelty appeals.

**Example:**
- Main heading: "How to write ads that sell"
- Subheading 1: "What the 5% best copywriters know..."
- Subheading 2: "The one element that determines success..."
- Subheading 3: "Why your competition will hate you..."

**Trade-offs:** Maintains interest in long copy; breaks up visual density; subheadings must deliver on promise.

---

## Call to Action Patterns

### The Urgency Trio
**Structure:** Combine three urgency stimulants: time limit + limited stock + price increase.

**Example:** "Order by Friday (time limit). Only 100 units available (scarcity). Price increases $50 next week (cost)."

**Trade-offs:** Maximizes urgency; can feel manipulative if overused; must be truthful.

---

### The Clear Direction Pattern
**Structure:** Remove all ambiguity from desired action; make it simple and specific.

**Example:** 
"Call 1-800-BENEFIT right now. Have your credit card ready. Allow 3 minutes."

Not: "Let us know if you're interested."

**Trade-offs:** Dramatically increases conversion; no wishy-washy language; requires specific way to respond.

---

### The Risk-Removal + Incentive Pattern
**Structure:** Eliminate hesitation (guarantee) + add incentive (bonus) together.

**Example:** "Order today and receive a free 30-day trial course ($197 value) + our 60-day money-back guarantee. You risk nothing."

**Trade-offs:** Most persuasive combo; requires genuine incentive; emphasize risk removal first, then bonus.

---

## Decision Rule Patterns

### The Copy Length Decision Tree

**Is product complex or high-involvement?**
- YES → Longer copy needed
- NO → Can use shorter copy

**Is product price high?**
- YES → Longer copy needed (more proof required)
- NO → Shorter copy often sufficient

**Is reader sophisticated?**
- YES → Can use technical details, dense proof
- NO → Need longer copy with clearer explanations

**Is the inherent appeal strong?**
- YES → Short copy can work
- NO → Longer copy needed to overcome objections

**Can you hold interest throughout?**
- YES → Go as long as needed to do all sales work
- NO → Cut ruthlessly, focus on strongest points

**Conclusion:** Copy length = length needed to perform complete sales work, never longer, never shorter.

---

### The Proof Selection Decision Tree

**Is reader skeptical?**
- YES → Use independent tests, expert endorsements, guarantees
- NO → Testimonials, demonstrations, reputation sufficient

**Is reader similar to your customers?**
- YES → Use customer testimonials (geographically close)
- NO → Use expert endorsements or tests (credibility required)

**Is benefit visual or experiential?**
- YES → Use demonstration pattern
- NO → Use testimonials, tests, guarantees

**Is risk/safety a concern?**
- YES → Lead with guarantee + expert endorsement
- NO → Emphasize results + reputation

**Can you demonstrate live?**
- YES → Demonstration is most persuasive
- NO → Use testimonials + tests + expert endorsement

## Gender-Specific Application Patterns

### Female Audience (Short-Term Psychology)
- Emphasize immediate benefits
- Highlight specific outcomes for specific situations
- Connect to daily life and relationships
- Show personal relevance first
- Use event-based reasoning (when this happens, use this)

**Example:** "When you're running late for meetings, this saves 15 minutes. Every day."

### Male Audience (Long-Term Psychology)
- Emphasize future benefits and long-term impact
- Focus on career development, security, advancement
- Show systematic improvement over time
- Connect to professional goals
- Use progression-based reasoning

**Example:** "This skill compounds over 5 years, positioning you for senior leadership roles."
